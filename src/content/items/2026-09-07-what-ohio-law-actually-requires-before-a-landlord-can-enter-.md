title: "What Ohio Law Actually Requires Before a Landlord Can Enter a Rental"
summary: "RL Property Management outlined Ohio's landlord entry rules, which are more specific than many Columbus owners expect. Ohio Revised Code 5321.04(A)(8) presumes 24 hours notice reasonable before a landlord enters an occupied unit, and entry must occur at a reasonable time for a legitimate purpose such as an inspection, repair or showing, with an emergency exception. Tenants have a matching duty under ORC 5321.05(B) not to unreasonably withhold consent. Getting entry wrong can expose an owner to damages, an injunction, attorney's fees or a terminated lease."
detail: "Ohio law sets a specific standard for when and how a landlord may enter an occupied rental, according to RL Property Management. ORC 5321.04(A)(8) states that 24 hours notice is presumed reasonable in the absence of evidence to the contrary, and entry must happen at a reasonable time and for a legitimate purpose, such as an inspection, a repair, an agreed service or a showing. The statute carves out an exception for emergencies or situations where giving notice is impracticable.\n\nTenants carry a corresponding obligation. Under ORC 5321.05(B), a resident cannot unreasonably withhold consent to an entry that otherwise meets the statute's requirements. When entry goes wrong, meaning notice was inadequate, the timing was unreasonable, or the purpose did not qualify, the company says the tenant's remedies include damages, an injunction, attorney's fees, and in some cases the right to terminate the lease outright.\n\nThe piece also draws a distinction Columbus owners may not expect: registry inspections conducted under the city's rental registration ordinance, Chapter 4515, only cover a property's exterior, common areas and shared mechanical systems. They do not authorize entry into occupied unit interiors, so a registry inspection does not substitute for the separate 24 hour notice process when an owner needs to get inside a leased unit itself.\n\nRL Property Management frames the guidance as informational rather than legal advice, noting Ohio law as of September 2026 and recommending owners with a specific dispute consult an Ohio attorney rather than rely on a general summary."
