title: "Landlord claims over COVID eviction freeze grow to 2,633, lawyer expects $1B payout"
summary: "The number of landlords seeking federal compensation for losses from the pandemic-era eviction moratorium has risen to 2,633 claims in Darby Development Co. v. United States, according to an amended complaint filed in the U.S. Court of Federal Claims in late August. Plaintiffs originally sought $26 billion, but co-counsel John McDermott told Multifamily Dive he expects the government to pay closer to $1 billion as the parties move toward settlement. Courts have ruled the CDC's eviction freeze was a physical taking of landlords' property. The window to join the Darby case has closed."
detail: "A federal court ruled in 2024, and reaffirmed in 2025, that the CDC's nearly yearlong pandemic eviction moratorium constituted a physical taking of landlords' property under the Fifth Amendment, because it removed a property owner's right to exclude a nonpaying tenant. That ruling opened the door to compensation claims, and the number of landlords joined to the lead case, Darby Development Co. v. United States, has grown to 2,633 as of a fourth amended complaint filed in late August.\n\nPlaintiffs' original filings sought $26 billion in restitution, but John McDermott, co-counsel for the plaintiffs at Dorsey & Whitney, told Multifamily Dive he expects an eventual settlement closer to $1 billion. Legal teams are now negotiating how much the government will pay.\n\nThe deadline for additional landlords to join the Darby case itself has passed. But attorney Anna Wills of Duane Morris said the ruling may still matter to landlords who operated in states with their own, stricter eviction moratoriums during the pandemic: those owners could have a separate takings claim against their state government, subject to that state's own statute of limitations, and should consult an eminent domain attorney to check."
why_it_matters: "A landlord who owned rental property during the pandemic-era eviction freeze and operated in a state with its own stricter moratorium may still have a separate claim against that state worth checking with an attorney, even though the window to join the federal Darby case has closed."
