title: "Ohio Supreme Court orders Trenton data center ban onto the ballot, sets precedent for petitions"
summary: "The Ohio Supreme Court ruled unanimously that Trenton, a city near Cincinnati, must let voters decide a proposed ban on new data centers after the city council rejected a citizen petition. Trenton's council had argued the petition needed signatures from 10% of all registered voters, but the court agreed with residents that the charter's language meant 10% of those who actually voted in the last general election, a far lower threshold. The court ordered a special election within 120 days of certification if the measure misses the fall ballot."
detail: "Five Trenton residents circulated a petition to amend the city charter and ban construction of new data centers, after two projects were proposed for the city and a third was under discussion. Trenton's charter requires a petition to be signed by 10% of the municipality's \"electors.\" The city council interpreted that as 10% of all 8,197 registered voters, meaning organizers needed 820 signatures. Residents argued it meant 10% of the 1,277 people who actually voted in the November 2025 general election, a threshold of 128 signatures they said they cleared more than twice over with 336 valid signatures.\n\nThe Ohio Supreme Court agreed with the residents in an unsigned opinion, finding \"clear and convincing evidence\" that the city council disregarded the applicable law when it refused to certify the petition. Because the ruling may come too late to make this fall's general ballot, the court ordered that a special election be held no later than 120 days after the petition is certified, which could put the question before Trenton voters as soon as November 3.\n\nThe ruling is the second the court has issued on a data center ballot petition in the past month, after a similar dispute in the village of Ashville, where officials had tried to bypass a public vote using emergency legislation. Both rulings narrow the room local governments have to keep data center proposals off the ballot once a valid petition is filed."
why_it_matters: "An investor or developer weighing a large project in an Ohio municipality that requires charter petitions should read electors to mean voters who cast ballots in the last general election, not all registered voters, since that is now the standard the state's highest court has enforced twice in a month."
